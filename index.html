What is VR?

60-minute lesson A-Frame & JavaScript VR Escape Room project
Relevant syllabus outcomes

Key terminology

Virtual Reality (VR)
A computer-generated environment that replaces most or all of the user’s view of the real world and allows them to experience and interact with a simulated space.
Augmented Reality (AR)
Technology that adds digital information or objects over a view of the real world rather than replacing it completely.
Mixed Reality (MR)
A combination of physical and digital environments where virtual objects can appear anchored to, and interact with, the real world.
Extended Reality (XR)
A broad term used to describe immersive technologies including VR, AR and MR.
Immersion
The extent to which a digital experience surrounds the user and reduces their awareness of the physical world.
Presence
The feeling that you are physically located inside a virtual environment, even though you know the environment is computer generated.

Read and understand

Virtual reality (VR) is a computing technology that places a user inside a computer-generated environment. Instead of viewing a digital world through a flat screen, the user wears a head-mounted display that presents slightly different images to each eye. The system continually updates the scene as the user's head moves, creating the impression that the virtual environment surrounds them. This sense of being located inside the digital space is called presence. Immersion describes how effectively the technology blocks out or replaces real-world sensory information and responds to the user's actions.

VR is different from augmented reality (AR) and mixed reality (MR). AR keeps the real world visible and places digital information over it, such as navigation arrows or a virtual object viewed through a phone camera. VR replaces most of the user's visual environment. Mixed reality sits between these ideas by allowing digital objects to appear anchored to and interact with the physical world. These technologies are often grouped under the broader term extended reality, or XR.

VR is used for much more than gaming. Surgeons can rehearse procedures, workers can practise dangerous tasks without real-world risk, architects can walk through buildings before construction, and students can explore environments that would otherwise be inaccessible. In every case, the usefulness of VR comes from combining software, 3D graphics, sensors, interaction design and data processing to create an experience that responds to the user.

In this unit you will not simply consume VR. You will design and program an interactive virtual environment using A-Frame, JavaScript and WebXR. Your final escape room will need to respond to player actions, remember progress, provide feedback and guide the user through a sequence of challenges. That means VR will be treated as a genuine computing solution rather than just a visual effect.

1. Examples

Worked example: Virtual museum tour.

A student puts on a headset and looks around a reconstructed ancient city. When the student turns their head, the view changes immediately. They can walk to an artefact and select it to reveal information.

This is VR because the real visual environment is replaced by a computer-generated one. It creates immersion by surrounding the user with digital content and presence because the user feels located inside the environment.

If the same artefact appeared on the student's real classroom desk through a phone camera, that would instead be AR .

Read and understand

A VR system works as a continuous input–process–output loop. Sensors inside the headset measure movement and orientation. Controllers or hand-tracking systems provide additional input about what the user is doing. Software processes this data, calculates the new position and direction of the virtual camera, then renders an updated image for the display. This loop repeats many times every second so the virtual world appears stable while the user moves.

Modern headsets commonly track six degrees of freedom. Three describe position: moving left and right, up and down, and forward and backward. Three describe rotation: turning, tilting and rolling the head. Tracking may combine accelerometers, gyroscopes and cameras. The software fuses these sensor readings to estimate the user's pose. Accurate tracking matters because even small errors can make virtual objects appear to drift or move incorrectly.

Latency is the delay between a user's movement and the corresponding change on the display. If this delay becomes noticeable, the visual information no longer matches the body's physical sense of movement. This can reduce immersion and may contribute to discomfort or cyber sickness. Frame rate is also important because the system must draw complex 3D scenes quickly and consistently. Developers therefore balance visual quality, object complexity and performance.

Thinking about VR as a system helps when debugging. If an interaction feels wrong, the cause may be the input method, the program logic, the rendered output or the timing between them. During this unit you will repeatedly identify inputs, processing decisions and outputs in your own escape-room systems so that complex behaviour can be broken into manageable parts.

1. Worked example

Worked example: turning your head in VR.

1. The headset's sensors detect that the user's head has rotated to the right.

2. The processor calculates the new head orientation.

3. The 3D camera is rotated to match.

4. The scene is rendered again from the new viewpoint.

5. The display shows the updated image.

This is a rapid input → process → output loop. It repeats many times every second.

Read and understand

VR creates safety issues that do not exist in the same way with ordinary desktop software. A user wearing a headset may not be able to see furniture, other people or obstacles in the room. Safe use therefore requires a clear physical play area, appropriate supervision and an understanding of the headset's boundary system. Experiences should also avoid requiring sudden uncontrolled movement, reaching beyond a safe area or actions that could cause users to collide with their surroundings.

Cyber sickness can occur when visual motion does not match what the body feels. For example, a virtual camera may move rapidly forward even though the user is physically standing still. Designers can reduce this risk by avoiding unnecessary camera movement, keeping frame rates stable, allowing users to control movement and providing stable visual reference points. Comfort is part of user-experience design, not an optional extra added at the end.

Immersive systems can also collect unusually detailed data. Head and hand movements, direction of gaze, voice, room mapping and interaction patterns can reveal information about a user. Developers need to consider why data is collected, how it is stored and who can access it. Ethical design means collecting only the information genuinely needed and clearly communicating how the system behaves.

Accessibility should influence design from the beginning. A VR experience that assumes every user can stand, reach with both hands, distinguish colours or hear audio cues may exclude people unnecessarily. Alternative controls, seated use, readable text, strong contrast and more than one form of feedback can make a solution usable by a wider audience. Your project will be evaluated partly on whether your interaction design is safe, clear and inclusive.

1. Worked example

Worked example: reducing cyber sickness.

Imagine a VR roller-coaster scene where the virtual camera suddenly spins while the user is standing still. The eyes report fast movement, but the inner ear reports no matching physical movement. This sensory conflict can cause discomfort.

A safer alternative is to let the user control movement, keep the camera stable when possible and avoid unnecessary forced rotation.

Read and understand

Before we begin creating virtual reality environments, we need to understand a little about how webpages are constructed. A-Frame uses HTML as the foundation for building 3D scenes.

HTML normally describes the structure of a webpage. It uses elements identified by tags. For example, a normal webpage might use a <p> element for a paragraph or an <h1> element for a heading.

A-Frame adds special HTML-style elements that represent objects in three-dimensional space. For example, <a-box> creates a box, <a-sphere> creates a sphere and <a-plane> can be used to create a floor or wall.

Every A-Frame virtual environment begins with an <a-scene>. Think of the scene as the container for the entire virtual world. The objects, lights, camera and interactive elements all belong inside this scene.

We control objects by adding attributes to their opening tags. 

<a-box position="1 1 -4" color="blue"></a-box>

In this example, <a-box> tells A-Frame which object to create. The position attribute tells A-Frame where to place the object and the color attribute controls its appearance.

When learning to code, one of the most useful habits is to change one thing at a time. Run the code first, make one small change, run it again and observe what changed. This makes programming much easier to understand and debug.

1. Worked example

Look carefully at this A-Frame element: 

<a-box position="1 1 -4" color="blue"></a-box>

We can break the line into smaller pieces.

  • <a-box> tells A-Frame to create a box.
  • position="1 1 -4" tells A-Frame where the box should appear.
  • color="blue" makes the box blue.
  • </a-box> closes the element.

Read and understand

A 3D environment uses three axes rather than the two axes used on a normal screen. The x-axis moves left and right, the y-axis moves up and down, and the z-axis moves forward and backward. In A-Frame, a position such as 2 1 -5 means the object is two units to the right of the origin, one unit above it and five units in front of the default camera. Understanding coordinates allows you to place objects deliberately instead of relying on trial and error.

Rotation is also represented with three values, measured in degrees. Rotating around the y-axis turns an object left or right, while rotation around x and z changes its tilt in other directions. Scale changes the size of an entity relative to its original dimensions. Because these properties combine, a single object can be positioned, turned and resized without changing its underlying geometry.

Spatial consistency matters in VR because the user experiences the scene at human scale. A door that looks acceptable on a laptop can feel far too small in a headset. Text that appears readable on a monitor may be difficult to read when it is several virtual metres away. Designers need to think about distance, reach, sightlines and the space required for movement.

Your escape room will eventually contain many entities, so organised coordinates are important. Plan a logical origin, keep the floor at a consistent height and use approximate real-world proportions. Deliberate spatial design makes later programming easier because you know where the camera, triggers, doors and puzzle objects should be relative to one another.

1. Worked example

Worked example: interpreting coordinates.

Object A has position="-2 1 -5" . This means it is 2 units left of the origin, 1 unit above the floor and 5 units in front of the camera.

If we change only the first value to 2 , the object moves to the right side of the scene. If we change the second to 3 , it rises. If we change -5 to -2, it moves closer to the camera.

Read and understand

Materials control how the surface of a 3D object appears. A material can define colour, transparency, roughness, metallic appearance and textures. Lighting then affects how those materials are perceived. Together, materials and lights create both the visual style of a scene and important information about which objects are significant or interactive.

Ambient light illuminates the whole scene evenly, while directional, point and spot lights create stronger highlights and shadows. Realistic lighting can make a scene more atmospheric, but realism is not always the best goal. If a puzzle object is hidden in darkness or an important button has the same colour and brightness as the wall behind it, the user may not understand what to do. Visual clarity is part of the interface.

The program does nothing until the click event occurs. When it does, the callback function runs and changes the entity. This is event-driven programming.

Read and understand

Today you are beginning to combine programming concepts rather than practising them separately. Read and understand

Read and understand

A storyboard represents the player's experience as a sequence of important moments. It can show where the player begins, what they notice, which interaction occurs, what feedback appears and how the next part of the experience becomes available. Storyboards are useful because they focus on the user's journey rather than only on the code.

A flowchart represents the algorithmic logic behind the same experience. Rectangles show processes, diamonds show decisions and arrows show the direction of control. A decision such as 'All keys collected?' can split the flow into different branches. This makes hidden program logic visible before implementation.

Escape-room puzzles often have dependencies. Puzzle B may require information produced by puzzle A, while the final challenge may require several independent tasks to be complete. Mapping these dependencies prevents impossible progression, circular logic and accidental shortcuts.

Planning does not mean the design can never change. It gives you a starting model that can be tested and revised. As development continues, update the flowchart when your logic changes so the documentation remains an accurate representation of the working solution.

Read and understand

User experience, or UX, describes the quality of a person's interaction with a system. In VR, UX includes much more than menus and buttons. It includes scale, movement, comfort, readability, spatial organisation, feedback, discoverability and how easily the player understands what to do next.

Wayfinding is the process of helping users understand where they are and how to move through an environment. Lighting, landmarks, doorways, colour and object placement can all guide attention. A strong escape room does not need to cover every wall with arrows; it designs the environment so the likely path feels understandable.

Diegetic interfaces are interface elements that appear to belong inside the virtual world. A countdown might appear on an in-world display, or a clue might be written on a virtual monitor. These can feel more immersive than a floating 2D overlay, but they still need to be readable and accessible.

Cognitive load is the amount of mental effort required to understand and use the system. A challenging puzzle can be good; confusion caused by inconsistent controls is not. Keep interaction rules consistent, provide feedback and avoid forcing the player to remember unnecessary information unrelated to the puzzle.

Read and understand

A linked puzzle is a set of interactions where the result of one changes what is possible in another. This requires the program to remember state. For example, solving a symbol puzzle may set symbolsSolved to true, allowing a previously locked keypad to accept input.

Dependencies can be represented using variables and selection. The program might test two conditions before opening the final door. This is more reliable than checking visual properties such as object colour because game state should be represented explicitly in the program.

As linked logic becomes more complex, use a state table. Record each variable, its initial value, which event changes it and which behaviours depend on it. This provides a map of the hidden data controlling the experience and makes unexpected behaviour easier to diagnose.

A good puzzle dependency should also make sense to the player. If solving one puzzle unexpectedly opens a door on the opposite side of the room with no feedback, the code may be correct but the experience is confusing. Connect programming logic with clear visual, textual or audio feedback.

Read and understand

An inventory is a way of representing items or achievements the player has collected. A simple game may only need a counter such as keysCollected. A more complex game may need an array that stores the names of specific items, such as ['redKey', 'map', 'battery'].

Arrays allow several related values to be stored in one structure. JavaScript provides methods for adding values, checking whether an item already exists and iterating through a collection. This is useful when the program needs to know exactly which objects have been found rather than just how many.

Collectables should be designed carefully. Selecting the same object repeatedly should not increase the count multiple times unless that behaviour is intended. The program can prevent duplicates by checking whether an item has already been collected before changing the inventory.

Feedback is essential. The player should understand that collection succeeded and, when appropriate, how much progress remains. This could be communicated through a small in-world display, a change in the object, a sound paired with a visual cue, or a clue that becomes available.

1. Worked example

Worked example: preventing duplicate collection. 

const inventory = [];

function collect(itemName) {
  if (!inventory.includes(itemName)) {
    inventory.push(itemName);
  }
}

The includes check prevents the same object from being counted repeatedly.

Read and understand

Read and understand

Systematic testing is different from casually trying the program. A test plan identifies specific features, inputs or actions, expected results and actual results. It should include normal use, incorrect sequences and edge cases. This creates evidence about reliability rather than relying on the statement 'it worked for me'.

An edge case is an unusual situation near the limits of expected behaviour. Examples include clicking the same key twice, attempting the final door before solving any puzzles, selecting two objects rapidly or reaching a timer value of zero. Edge cases often reveal logic problems that normal testing misses.

Regression testing is performed after a change to check that features which previously worked still behave correctly. A small modification to shared code can accidentally break another puzzle. Re-running important test cases after changes reduces this risk.

Testing is most useful when failures are recorded accurately. Do not change an expected result simply because the program behaved differently. A failed test identifies useful work. Prioritise defects that block progression, corrupt game state or create serious UX problems before minor visual imperfections.

Read and understand

Debugging is the process of finding and correcting the cause of incorrect behaviour. A symptom is what you observe; the cause is the underlying problem. For example, a door failing to open is a symptom. The cause might be an incorrect ID, a variable that never changes or a condition that tests the wrong value.

Syntax errors occur when code breaks the grammatical rules of the language. Runtime errors occur while the program is executing. Logic errors are more difficult because the program runs but produces the wrong result. Each type requires a different debugging approach.

The browser console is a valuable tool. console.log() can display variable values and messages at key points in the program. This lets you trace whether an event occurred, whether a function ran and what state the program contained at that moment.

Change one thing at a time. First reproduce the problem reliably, then isolate the smallest relevant part of the code, inspect state, make one hypothesis and test one change. Randomly editing several sections at once makes it difficult to know which change fixed or created the problem.

Usability testing examines how real users interact with the solution. The goal is not to prove that the developer's design is correct; it is to discover where the design does not match the user's expectations. This is why testers should receive a clear goal but not step-by-step instructions for solving the room.

Observation produces valuable evidence. Record where the user hesitates, what they try to interact with, whether they notice feedback and where they become stuck. Comments provide qualitative data, while measures such as completion time, number of errors or number of hints provide quantitative data.

Testing should involve more than one user because individual behaviour varies. If several users misunderstand the same interaction, that is strong evidence of a design problem. If only one user has difficulty, investigate further before assuming the interface must change.

After testing, prioritise improvements by impact and effort. Fix problems that stop users progressing or cause major confusion first. User-testing evidence should appear in your project documentation so your final evaluation can explain how the solution changed in response to real feedback.

Read and understand

Inclusive design asks whether unnecessary barriers have been created by the solution. A technically functioning program can still fail users if text is too small, colours are indistinguishable, objects require excessive reach or instructions are available only through audio.

Read and understand

A development sprint is a focused period of work aimed at completing a defined set of priorities. Near the end of a project, the most important skill is often deciding what not to build. New features introduce new code and therefore new opportunities for defects.
